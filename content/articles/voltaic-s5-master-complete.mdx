Recently, I hit Master Complete on the Voltaic Season 5 benchmarks.

Accomplishing this was a gratifying milestone, as Master Complete was the long-term goal I had set my eyes on back when I first started aim training. In the grand scheme of things, Master Complete definitely isn't the be-all and end-all - leaderboards place it around the top 2-3% across the categories. However, reaching it genuinely feels like I finally "completed the tutorial," and any further progression would mean stepping into apex-tier territory.

![My Voltaic S5 Master Complete benchmark table](/media/articles/voltaic-s5-master-complete/benchmarks.png)

It was a cool experience to cross this threshold right around the time I turned 30. Having started aim training at 27, this grind reaffirmed my belief that age really isn't a physical barrier when it comes to acquiring motor skills. If anything, balancing dedicated practice with the mental fatigue of daily adult responsibilities is far harder than getting your hands to move precisely.

Part of what made this climb special to me was doing it "my way," as I alternated between lightweight mice and decently fast skates on ultra-fast glasspads throughout the grind. I have a decent amount of peripheral experience and know firsthand that my progression was much faster whenever I used a more traditional and balanced cloth setup. But since I’ve always been a bit of a rebel, I stubbornly stuck to my preferred setup anyway. As a result, I can gladly say this was accomplished on my own terms.

Anyhow, I wanted to document these thoughts before the details faded, partly as a personal retrospective, and partly in case it's useful to anyone looking to go down a similar path.

## Background

To give some context, my background isn't actually in FPS games, but rather League of Legends. In League, I was decently high-ranked in S3 back when I lived in China, and eventually played, coached, and managed my university's teams in the US. However, I've always considered myself more of a thinking player rather than someone with good mechanics.

This carried over when I started playing FPS games as well, though my experience there was fairly sparse - mostly full-stack parties in CS and Apex, and primarily Valorant in recent years. I always felt that raw aim was a notable weak point for me, which is likely why the structured nature of aim training appealed to me so much.

I was loosely aware of KovaaK's for a while (I remember reading an old Aimer7 guide back in the day), but my real entry point was watching the Red Bull Ready Check event a little over two years ago. It was awesome seeing an entire community built around mechanical improvement that still felt relatively underground.

![The Red Bull Ready Check event](/media/articles/voltaic-s5-master-complete/redbull-ready-check.webp)

And from there onwards, down the rabbit hole I went! I started out on Aimlabs, placing around Gold on the Voltaic S3 benchmarks. I was genuinely hooked on the process - I'd leave work every day excited to boot up Aimlabs and actively look up video guides and resources to absorb information from. I hit Platinum shortly after, and Diamond after a consistent stretch of grinding, eventually switching over to Kovaak's as that seemed to be the more flexible option.

![Progression through Gold, Platinum, and Diamond](/media/articles/voltaic-s5-master-complete/voltbot-progression.png)

Then real life hit. I got laid off and had to pivot almost all my mental bandwidth into technical interview prep. Between staring at code all day and dealing with the mental exhaustion of interviewing, quality sessions just weren't happening. Most of 2025 ended up being an unintended maintenance phase.

Once I finally landed a job and the stress settled, I was able to refocus. I spent some time going through the Viscose S1 benchmarks, rediscovered how much fun KovaaK's could be, and eventually channeled that momentum into the recent push for the Voltaic Season 5 benchmarks.

I'm not quite sure what the trigger was, but after camping around Diamond/Jade and casually playing through the Viscose benchmarks, I had an idea to make a push for the VT S5 benches around mid-May, just to test the waters and see where I could go. I gave the static clicking scenarios a try and surprisingly, around an hour in, I ended up hitting Master on 1w3ts, and then after another 40 minutes on ww3t as well - even while trying out fingertip mice throughout the process. This gave me the confidence to set my sights on Master Complete and commit to a full grind.

My routine during this period was straightforward, if not a bit extreme. I’d target a scenario, start with the related VDIM scenarios to warm up, then continuously grind that scenario until I either hit the score or was too exhausted to keep going. It was honestly quite shocking how much tolerance I had for longer hours. Previously, I would mostly stop at an hour max for a single session (probably closer to 20-40 minute sessions on average), but for some reason this time around, I was able to sit down and repeatedly grind for hours on end.

Is brute-forcing 3-4+ hour sessions of a single scenario the optimal way to improve technique? Probably not. But for the first time in a while, I was having genuine fun sitting down and obsessing over the grind.

Looking back, this stretch made me realize how much I used to overthink things. It’s remarkably easy to fall into the trap of trying to intellectually solve every flaw in your technique, when in reality, the answer is often as simple as just committing to the reps.

For the majority of the benchmarks, this approach didn't feel completely random - I could actively feel improvement as I tried to address flaws in my technique. There were only a handful of scenarios where I felt like I was hitting a complete brick wall, though that did make the final breakthrough feel that much better.

As I mentioned before, static clicking came pretty quickly. The fingertip mice I was experimenting with only took about a week to really adjust to, likely thanks to my prior experience playing osu! on a tablet. The added fingertip mobility also helped quite a bit with the vertical target movement in this scenario.

For the longest time, the main issue with my static clicking was the unstable and inaccurate flicks, but I always thought slowing down to try to 'clean up' my aim was the right approach. What I eventually realized is that I was obsessing over how my aim looked rather than how it actually worked, and I was setting a limiter on myself because of this.

Technique-wise, I credit the most progress to trying out slower sensitivities (60-80cm). I've always played on mid-fast sensitivities for basically every game, and it took some self-reflection to realize that because of this, I was using my wrist for both initial flicks and microcorrections, which was the root cause of the stability issues I was facing. Playing in a slower range forced more shoulder and arm engagement, and even after I swapped back to faster sensitivities, the experience left me much more conscious of which muscle groups I was engaging. This is what helped my pacing throughout each run the most.

The other big improvement was my click confirmation. I've historically had issues with spam clicking, which was another reason I kept wanting to slow down, but slowing down always ruined pacing and led to lower scores. The breakthrough here came from the grind itself - I had a bad tendency to move my eyes to the next target before actually confirming and clicking the current one, which was hurting my accuracy. So instead, I focused on keeping my eyes on the current target until I visually saw my crosshair overlap, or until I was confident I could accurately micro-correct onto it even without the visual confirmation. Over the 40 minutes of grinding, my score improved substantially as I got better at this.

**What's next**

- Flick accuracy could still use work.
- Continue improving in both avenues that I described, still far from perfect.

Ground was the third hardest scenario for me. Part of this was because I had just come back from a week-long trip to Japan and swapped to the Lycan halfway through, but I also had to iron out a lot of issues with my technique and approach to see progress here. I came in feeling decently confident as I had a Jade score in the Aimlabs S3 benches, but I realized halfway through that I had a lack of actual experience with tracking varied ground movements in games, since I never really played enough games with movements like this or had enough experience with varied Ground scenarios in aim trainers. Grinding this out took a few consecutive days of just playing Ground, which was incredibly draining in hindsight.

Similar to Aether, a lot of my early time was just spent throwing myself at the scenario without thinking too much, but when I realized I was hardly progressing, I started looking through the Raw Input and Voltaic Discord servers for any advice for tackling Ground. I came across a post that advised tackling each bot with different muscle groups, which was eye-opening to me, as I had just been leading every bot with my arm and leading direction changes with my wrist.

For Bot 1, I actually kept the existing approach - because the bot infrequently changed directions and had the occasional blink, I would mostly lead with my arm for speed matching and flicks, and then use my wrist to guide directional changes.

Bot 2 had more frequent direction changes, and because of this, I led with my wrist more and used my arm for following through movements.

Bot 3 felt the weirdest - the bot was slower but had way more variety, so sometimes it would do a long continuous motion, and other times it would do an S-shape motion and require a more vertical motion. This really threw me off, mainly because I've always struggled with track-stop scenarios, and the S-shaped movements mimic that with sudden drops in horizontal movement. Surprisingly, engaging my fingertips helped the most here. The bot was slow enough that I could follow the general movement with my wrist without too much tension, and when I led movements with my fingertips, I was able to react better to the varied patterns.

**What's next**

- To be honest, I'm not too confident if this was the intended or right way to approach this scenario, so I want to refine this approach and see if it still holds up in Advanced.
