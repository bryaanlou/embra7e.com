**How did this mousepad end up on my desk?** To be completely honest, it shouldn't even be here yet. The minute the Ryo launched, I placed an order from the first batch, fully prepared to wait until June to receive the mousepad. However, just a few days later, I received a shipping notification in my inbox — it turns out, due to a logistical mix-up, a sample intended for an actual reviewer got rerouted straight to me. The owner(s) were awesome here, immediately jumping in to help me figure out what happened and reassuring me that my original pre-order was unaffected. Once that confusion was cleared up, I was just simply ecstatic to be able to try out the pad a full month earlier than expected.

Before breaking down my perspective on the pad, it might help to lay out how my preferences evolved to this point to give you a baseline.

## My background

I got into glass pads three years ago starting with the Skypad 3.0, and have slowly expanded my collection over the years.

![Mousepad collection laid out side by side](/images/articles/kyomi-ryo/IMG_9445.JPEG)

Ironically, my aiming background is primarily in playing tac FPS and Kovaak's — the polar opposite of where glass surfaces are typically recommended. I do agree with the overall sentiment and think slower, cloth surfaces are better for tactical shooters due to the heavy emphasis on stability (story for another day!), but my interest in glass pads simply went beyond caring enough about optimizing my casual play in Valorant.

I was on some random muddy desk mat before I knew anything about peripherals, and the Skypad 3.0 was my entry into glass — a smooth, fast surface with minimal texture, where the idea was "with enough raw mouse control, you'd have effortless glide and infinite potential". Sounds appealing, right? Well, after a few months of using the pad, my thoughts definitely changed quite a bit.

## Challenges

The main problem I faced was with stability. This came from two areas — the lack of stopping power, as well as the lack of feedback, which combined, led to issues like wobbly unstable flicks. Even holding angles felt like they took conscious effort — any tiny inkling of a twitch and my mouse would move. You might have also heard that glass feels 'floaty,' — I definitely felt this firsthand. To someone who isn't familiar, imagine the feeling of making a flick on a regular mousepad, and then contrast that with the feeling of trying to flick an air hockey pusher. Instead of being able to lean heavily into your mousepad to help you naturally stop during flicks, you essentially have to apply your own pressure to come to a stop. All of that led me to feel like I wasn't being supported by the mousepad. I even found myself subconsciously avoiding flicks, which led to a huge slow down in my static clicking progression.

## Finding the right fit

I eventually got frustrated enough and made the decision to hop off glass just to see what options were out there. I ended up trying out quite a few different cloth pads, such as the Artisan Key-83, Artisan Raiden, Freefall SVBC+ to name a few. Testing these was an eye opener, especially to the value of texture. With texture, every movement would create feedback, leading to more conscientiousness in knowing where my mouse was. This helped immensely with wider motions and gave me back some of the confidence I had lost with clicking scenarios. A few months in, I started performing noticeably better and also noticed steady improvement in places that I originally was plateau'd. However, despite all of the positives I was feeling, the memory I had of moving onto the Skypad for the first time just kept replaying in my head — I really just missed the glass experience.

And so, likely to the detriment of my own progression, I went back. I discovered that there were actually glass pads with the texture that I enjoyed so much from my cloth pads out there, eventually discovering and purchasing the Padsmith Rosa. The Rosa made me realize that a lot of the issues I had weren't necessarily generic to glass pads as a whole — many were due to characteristics that those pads had — specifically the lack of tangible feedback. Using the Rosa was amazing — I felt like I retained a lot of the pros as well as my performance from cloth, while not compromising on the glide experience on glass. Admittedly though, it did leave me curious about what this profile would look like with extreme speed, and coincidentally around that time, I started learning about newer speed focused offerings hitting the market from Chinese brands like Abyssgaming and Dysphoria.

Their releases introduced me to a very specific profile: extreme speed mixed with heavy texture and also the addition of an extra feature — push-in slowdown. Push-in slowdown was not something I would have imagined could coexist with an extremely fast profile. Essentially, by applying pressure into the pad, you could slow down the speed, similar to softer cloth pads. This was incredibly useful in sudden wide flicks and ultimately added another layer of control over mouse movements.

This niche fits my use case perfectly — the raw speed is simply just plain fun to use, the texture gives me real confidence during wider motions, providing awareness of where my mouse is on the pad, and the push-in slowdown acts as a safety net, helping immensely with large, sudden movements and stops. That realization led me straight to the Abyssgaming Lanyu/Veil, which was my absolute favorite pad before the Kyomi Ryo came out. The Lanyu held that top spot because it leaned heavily into its push-in slowdown, giving me a massive braking mechanism on a profile that was incredibly fast.

## Enter the Ryo

Having the Lanyu as my daily driver meant I went into the Kyomi Ryo with a very specific set of expectations. I wanted to see how it handled that specific profile of texture, speed, and push down control.

When the pre-production sample landed on my desk, the physical specs were the first thing to look at:

- **Dimensions (500 × 450 mm):** A bit larger than your standard 490 × 420 mm footprint. It's a subtle size increase that I appreciate for the extra space for vertical movements.
- **Edges (3rd Gen Edgeless):** Features a premium 2.5D rounding that completely eliminates sharp edges and feels incredible on the wrist.
- **Profile (2.2 mm spec / ~2.3 mm on my end):** Easily one of the thinnest glass pads in my rotation, right on par with recent drops from Dysphoria and Abyssgaming.
- **Base (Custom Silicone Grid):** Similar to the Wallhack SP-004's grid structure, but the execution here is completely uniform. It lays down completely flat, contrasting sharply with the deeper concave dips in the SP-004's grids.

(Note: The retail kit is slated to include a 40-pack of Kyomi Bubble dot skates and other misc accessories, but my reviewer copy only arrived with the pad and a microfiber cloth.)

## Design & Aesthetics

Visually, I absolutely adore the art style. The print features a cold-toned watercolor style utilizing deep blues, silvers, and purples, standing out sharply from the sea of anime-style pads for a much more distinct look. On the desk, it projects an incredibly dreamy, ethereal presence. I did notice that there wasn't an artist credited, so I reached out to the owners, who promptly got back and credited the artwork to @weweired9. I also did reach out to the artist to try to confirm this, but I haven't heard back. If you're like me and appreciate this style of art though, unfortunately I've heard that Kyomi's next release will lean back toward an anime aesthetic, which is quite a bummer.

![Detail of the Ryo's watercolor artwork featuring deep blues and silvers](/images/articles/kyomi-ryo/IMG_9415.JPEG)

## Surface Profile

The Ryo features a very dry, heavily textured, large-grained surface profile. It has a pronounced physical roughness that you can easily feel under your fingertips and audibly hear when moving across it, without any sort of abrasiveness.

### Moisture & Skin Stick

With completely dry skin, the surface performs well. Unfortunately, the moment even a small amount of moisture or sweat is introduced, the texture becomes incredibly grabby. While this won't matter to the vast majority of the community who already use arm sleeves on glass, it's a definitive buyer-beware if you are one of those rare people who prefer playing sleeveless.

## Performance

With the specs out of the way, how does the mousepad perform compared to something like the Lanyu that I was on before?

What I found is that while the Ryo and the Lanyu occupy a similar niche, they tilt the scales differently: the Ryo turns the overall speed profile up a notch and trades away that immediate, aggressive push-in stop for a more progressive, tasteful deceleration.

- **Static Friction:** Low. It's noticeably faster than the Lanyu and sits on par with the fastest mousepad in my collection — the Special Gear Autumn Whispers.
- **Dynamic Friction:** Low but less floaty, due to the deceleration from pushing in.
- **Push-in:** Applying downward pressure engages a distinct slowing mechanic. Crucially, it yields a smoother and more progressive deceleration rather than the instant stop characteristic of the Lanyu.

### Wide Motions

During larger sweeping motions, the physical grain of the surface provides heavy, highly tactile feedback directly through your mouse skates. I love this because of the aforementioned benefit of flick awareness that textured surfaces give. Applying pressure toward the end of flicks gives you a tasteful amount of mechanical control to smoothly decelerate where you want to land.

This also avoids the stop-start, disconnected feel that you get from a pad like the Lanyu when trying to fluidly chain movements together. Because the Lanyu forces such an abrupt, hard stop, you easily lose all your momentum, making it clunky to start a new motion again from a dead halt. With the Ryo, since the deceleration is progressive, it's incredibly easy to pick that motion back up or redirect it mid-glide before the mouse comes to a complete stop. It makes chaining movements feel fluid and connected.

### Micro-Adjustments

Conversely, smaller micros quiet down that physical texture significantly. When you aren't actively digging into the surface for a massive stop, fine micro-corrections transition into a much smoother feel.

The exact behavior here is more or less determined by your skate choice. Skates that reduce the overall feedback from the texture give you differing levels of smoothness during micros, whereas options that emphasize the texture dig straight into the pad to preserve that raw, tactile feedback.

## Skates

Note: A lot of skates start out incredibly rough because of the Ryo's texture, but they smooth out once broken in, so keep in mind first impressions may not fully reflect the whole experience.

![Skates and accessories tested on the Ryo, including Manta Ray dots and Orange skates](/images/articles/kyomi-ryo/IMG_9449.JPEG)

- **Westlab Offerings (Orange, Black, Blue, Creams):** I spent most of my time testing with the Oranges, and they were easily the smoothest option out of the bunch. They feature a very fast static and dynamic profile (admittedly felt unwieldy at times) but offer a great blend: wider flicks still retain a more muted texture feedback, while smaller micro-adjustments become quite smooth. The other Westlab variations all felt great as well, offering slightly different speed profiles depending on your preference.
- **Dynamic Manta Rays:** Low static and medium-low dynamic friction. They don't completely smooth out the texture but give off a lower frequency feel under your hand (if this makes any sense). They also feel a bit more stop-starty being more sensitive to push in.
- **Kirisame Simo:** Low static and medium dynamic friction. Maintains the rough texture in larger movements but is a lot smoother in smaller micros.

## Final Thoughts

Ultimately, transitioning from the Lanyu to the Ryo forced an adjustment in how I approached my shots. Because of the noticeable step up in raw speed, as well as the shift from a sharp stop to a more extended slowdown from push in, I had to spend time consciously softening up my flicks, learning to rely on a smoother deceleration rather than the instant hard stops I was accustomed to. That said, the slowdown is still nothing like the floatiness you'd get from pads without any push-in at all.

The deceleration mechanics are really just one piece of what makes this pad work though — the Kyomi Ryo stands out because of how cohesive the entire package is as a very fast, heavily textured surface that delivers incredible tactile feedback. That performance is matched by a premium build and an amazing looking watercolor style print.

Moving forward, I'll be keeping an eye on how the coating holds up over time and how the final mass-production units perform compared to this sample, but if the specific performance profile I've described here sounds like your cup of tea, I really don't think you'd be disappointed. It's a specialized and incredibly well-executed hard pad that I find entirely worth it.
