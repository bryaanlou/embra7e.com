**How did this mousepad end up on my desk?** To be completely honest, it shouldn't even have gotten here yet. The minute the first batch of the Kyomi Ryo launched, I placed an order, fully prepared to wait until the official shipping date in June to receive the mousepad. However, just a few days later, I received a shipping notification in my inbox. It turns out, due to a logistical mix-up, a sample intended for an actual reviewer got rerouted straight to me. The owner(s) immediately jumped in to help me figure out what happened and reassured me that my original pre-order was unaffected. Once that confusion was cleared up, I was simply ecstatic to be able to try out the pad a full month earlier than expected.

For context, this isn't my first glass pad — I've accumulated a handful over the years and figured I'd tap into that experience to share some honest thoughts on the Ryo. Of course, everyone looks for different things in their peripherals, so to give you a baseline, here's how my preferences have evolved over the years.

![Glasspad collection laid out side by side](/media/articles/kyomi-ryo/IMG_9445.JPEG)

## My background

My aiming background is primarily in Kovaak's and tac FPS, which is quite ironic considering tac FPS games are the polar opposite of where glass surfaces are typically recommended due to the heavy emphasis on stability and consistency. This is actually a sentiment I do agree with (story for another day), but my interest in glass pads simply went beyond caring enough about optimizing my casual play in Valorant.

I was on some random muddy desk mat before I knew anything about peripherals, and the Skypad 3.0 was my entry into glass — a smooth, fast surface with minimal texture, where the idea was "with enough raw mouse control, you'd have effortless glide and infinite potential." This sounded incredibly appealing for someone looking for a new experience, and I quickly placed an order. As soon as it arrived, I was mesmerized by the feeling of moving my mouse around. The level of speed that the Skypad 3.0 offered was quite literally night and day from my previous desk mat. This feeling of novelty lasted for a few months, but unfortunately the experience wasn't all positive.

## Challenges

Swapping to glass came with a slew of challenges. Many of these I knew about and made the conscious decision to swap regardless, as they were supposedly fixable through an adjustment period and active aim training. Even after a few months though, issues persisted. The main challenge I faced was with stability, which came from two areas: the lack of stopping power as well as the lack of feedback. Combined, these led to issues such as wobbly unstable flicks — even holding angles felt like they took conscious effort! Any tiny inkling of a twitch and my mouse would move. You might have also heard that glass feels 'floaty.' I definitely felt this firsthand. For those who aren't familiar, imagine flicking your mouse on a regular cloth mousepad, and then contrast that with the feeling of trying to flick an air hockey pusher. Instead of being able to lean into the mousepad to stop during flicks, you essentially have to brake yourself by applying force in the opposite direction of motion. All of the above led me to feel like I wasn't necessarily being supported by the mousepad. I even found myself subconsciously avoiding flicks, which led to a huge slow down in my static clicking progression.

## Finding the right fit

I eventually got frustrated enough and made the decision to hop off glass just to see what options were out there. I ended up trying out quite a few different cloth pads, such as the Artisan Key-83, Artisan Raiden, Freefall SVBC+ to name a few. Testing these was an eye opener, especially to the concept of texture. With texture, every movement would create feedback, leading to more conscientiousness in knowing where my mouse was throughout every movement. This helped immensely with wider motions and gave back some of the confidence I had lost with clicking scenarios. A few months in, I started performing noticeably better and also saw steady improvement in places that I originally plateau'd. However, despite all the positives, the memory I had of moving onto the Skypad for the first time left too much of an impression — I really just missed the glass experience.

And so, likely to the detriment of my own progression, I went back. I discovered that there were actually glass pads with the texture that I enjoyed so much from cloth pads, such as the Padsmith Rosa. Using the Rosa was amazing! I felt like I retained my performance as well as a lot of the pros from cloth, while not compromising on the glide experience on glass. The Rosa made me realize that a lot of the issues I had weren't necessarily generic to glass pads as a whole — many were due to characteristics those pads had, such as the lack of tangible feedback from smooth surfaces.

![Padsmith Rosa texture](/media/articles/kyomi-ryo/IMG_9494.JPEG)

Admittedly though, it did leave me curious about what this profile would look like with extreme speed. Coincidentally around that time, I started learning about newer speed focused offerings hitting the market from Chinese brands like Abyssgaming and Dysphoria.

Their releases introduced me to a very specific profile: extreme speed mixed with heavy texture and the addition of an extra feature — push-in slowdown. Push-in slowdown was not something I would have imagined could coexist with extreme speed on glass. Essentially, by applying pressure into the pad, you could slow down the speed of your movements, similar to leaning into the plushiness of softer cloth pads. This is incredibly useful in situations where you need to make sudden wide flicks, ultimately adding another layer of control over mouse movements.

This niche fits my use case perfectly — the raw speed is just plain fun to use, the texture gives me confidence especially during wider motions, and the push-in slowdown adds another vector of control, helping immensely with sudden movements and stops. This realization led me straight to the Abyssgaming Lanyu/Veil, which had all of these characteristics. The Lanyu was my absolute favorite pad before the Kyomi Ryo came out because it leaned heavily into its push-in slowdown, giving me a massive braking mechanism on a pad that was also incredibly fast. Having the Lanyu as my daily driver meant I went into the Kyomi Ryo with a very specific set of expectations. I wanted to see how it handled that combination of texture, speed, and push-down control.

## Enter the Ryo

When the pre-production sample landed on my desk, the physical specs were the first thing to look at:

- **Dimensions (500 × 450 mm):** A bit larger than the standard 490 × 420 mm sizing. It's a subtle size increase that I appreciate for the extra space for vertical movements.
- **Edges (3rd Gen Edgeless):** Features a premium 2.5D rounding that completely eliminates sharp edges and feels incredible on the wrist.
- **Profile (2.2 mm spec / ~2.3 mm on my end):** Easily one of the thinnest glass pads in my rotation, right on par with recent drops from Dysphoria and Abyssgaming.
- **Base (Custom Silicone Grid):** Similar to the Wallhack SP-004's grid structure, but the execution here is more uniform. It lays down completely flat, contrasting with the deeper concave dips in the SP-004's grids.

## Design & aesthetics

Visually, I absolutely adore the art style. The print features a cold-toned watercolor style utilizing deep blues, silvers, and purples, standing out sharply from the sea of anime-style pads for a much more distinct look. On the desk, it projects an incredibly dreamy, ethereal presence. I did notice that there wasn't an artist credited, so I asked the owners, who promptly got back and credited the artwork to [@weweired9](https://x.com/weweired9). I also reached out to the artist to try and confirm this from their end as well, but I haven't heard back. If you're like me and appreciate this style of art, I've heard that Kyomi's next release will lean back toward a more mainstream anime aesthetic, which is quite the bummer.

## Surface profile

The Ryo features a very dry, heavily textured, large-grained surface profile. It has a pronounced physical roughness that you can easily feel under your fingertips and audibly hear when moving across it, though it never feels abrasive.

### Moisture & skin stick

With completely dry skin, the surface performs well. Unfortunately, the moment even a small amount of moisture or sweat is introduced, the texture becomes incredibly grabby. While this won't matter to the vast majority of the community who already use arm sleeves on glass, it's a definitive buyer-beware if you are one of those rare people who prefer playing sleeveless.

## Performance

With the specs out of the way, how does the mousepad perform compared to something like the Lanyu that I was on before?

What I found is that while the Ryo and the Lanyu occupy a similar niche, they tilt the scales differently: the Ryo turns the overall speed profile up a tier and runs a drier and rougher surface.

- **Static Friction:** Low. It's noticeably faster than the Lanyu and sits on par with the fastest mousepad in my collection, the Special Gear Autumn Whispers.
- **Dynamic Friction:** Low but it feels less floaty, due to the deceleration from pushing in.
- **Push-in:** Applying downward pressure engages a distinct slowing mechanic. The deceleration is smooth and progressive.

### Speed vs Control

Historically, manufacturers have addressed the tradeoff between speed and control by tweaking either the static or dynamic friction. Both approaches come with their pros and cons.

Increasing **static friction** gives you more control during large motions while keeping precision in micros via low dynamic friction, but it introduces a stop-start, disconnected feel — recovering from a flick and chaining into a new motion gets clunky because you need more force to start moving again.

Increasing **dynamic friction** sidesteps that issue by giving you a smoother deceleration to lean into. But tuning the dynamic friction usually kills the speed entirely, as low dynamic friction is what makes smaller mouse movements feel fast and responsive.

The Ryo takes a different approach: keep both static and dynamic friction low, and add a third dimension via push-in that engages the slowdown only when you ask for it. That lets you consciously decide when to lean into the pad for sudden stopping power and when to lean off the pressure to chain movements smoothly, without compromising on the underlying speed profile.

### Wide motions

How the Ryo's design plays out in practice depends on the size of the movement you're making. During larger sweeping motions, the physical grain of the surface provides heavy, highly tactile feedback directly through your mouse skates. I love this because of the aforementioned benefit of flick awareness that textured surfaces give. Applying pressure toward the end of flicks gives you a tasteful amount of control to smoothly decelerate where you want to land.

### Micro-adjustments

Conversely, smaller micros quiet down that physical texture significantly. When you aren't actively digging into the surface for a massive stop, fine micro-corrections transition into a much smoother feel.

The exact behavior here is more or less fine-tunable by your skate choice. Skates that reduce the overall feedback from texture give you differing levels of smoothness during micros, whereas options that emphasize the texture dig straight into the pad to preserve that raw, tactile feedback. The same is the case for the level of push-in that you want. Some skate options lean into the push-in for more stopping power, while others preserve that floaty feel by reducing the amount.

## Skates

*Note: A lot of skates start out incredibly rough because of the Ryo's texture, but they smooth out once broken in, so keep in mind first impressions may not fully reflect the whole experience.*

![Skates and accessories tested on the Ryo, including Manta Ray dots and Orange skates](/media/articles/kyomi-ryo/IMG_9449.JPEG)

- **Westlab Offerings (Orange, Black, Blue, Creams):** I spent most of my time testing with the Oranges, and they were easily the smoothest option out of the bunch. They feature a very fast static and dynamic profile (admittedly felt unwieldy at times) but offer a great blend: wider flicks still retain a more muted texture feedback, while smaller micro-adjustments become quite smooth. The other Westlab variations all felt great as well, offering slightly different speed profiles depending on your preference.
- **Dysphoria Manta Rays:** Low static and medium-low dynamic friction. They don't completely smooth out the texture but give off a lower frequency feel under your hand (if this makes any sense). They also feel a bit more stop-starty being more sensitive to push in.
- **Kirisame Simo:** Low static and medium dynamic friction. Maintains the rough texture in larger movements but is a lot smoother in smaller micros.

## Final thoughts

Ultimately, transitioning to the Ryo forced an adjustment in how I approached my mouse movements. Because of the noticeable step up in raw speed, I had to consciously soften up my motions while also learning to rely on the pad's push-in when I needed control.

The deceleration mechanics are really just one piece of what makes this pad work though. The Kyomi Ryo stands out as a very fast, heavily textured surface that provides incredible tactile feedback, delivering a very cohesive package whose performance is also matched by a premium build and an amazing looking watercolor style print.

Moving forward, I'll be keeping an eye on how the coating holds up over time and how the final mass-production unit compares to this sample, but if the specific performance profile I've described here sounds like your cup of tea, I really don't think you'd be disappointed. It's a specialized and incredibly well-executed glass pad that I find entirely worth it.
